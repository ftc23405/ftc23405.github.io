DECODE 2025–26

We started the season as a close-range shooter with no indexing. We chose to challenge ourselves and pursue the far launch zone instead — to offer differentiated value and create real synergy with our alliance partners.

Season progression

Four meets, four robots

Nothing here was right the first time.

Worked

  • Could block other robots from scoring
  • Fast drivetrain on 435 RPM motors

Didn't

  • Couldn't hold three artifacts
  • Shooter inconsistent and broke apart easily
  • Transfer inconsistent; flywheel ripped apart often
  • Drivetrain bevel gears kept coming apart

Takeaway: fast cycle times matter more than raw capability. We rebuilt around a ramp and a simple double-motor flywheel shooter.

Worked

  • Held three artifacts
  • Reliable shooting
  • Intake funnel guided artifacts in

Didn't

  • Artifacts fell out after intaking through the shooter
  • Couldn't shoot far consistently
  • Transfer had dead spots
  • Slow intake; couldn't reach corner artifacts

Takeaway: the straight ramp bought us distance but cost us contact time with the artifact.

Worked

  • Stored three artifacts well
  • Efficient transfer, far fewer jams
  • Could shoot from far and close

Didn't

  • Shooting still inconsistent
  • Repeated robot disconnection issues
  • Inconsistent autonomous

Takeaway: adding a lip to the ramp fixed the shooting geometry. Consistency was now a software problem, not a hardware one.

Key features

  • Reliable shooting with Limelight auto-aim
  • Consistent scoring from the far launch zone
  • Reworked transfer that indexes one artifact at a time
  • Consistent autonomous built on Pedro Pathing

Result: 3rd Place Inspire Award at the Feynman League Tournament.

Subsystems

Every mechanism is a version number

Here's how each one got where it ended up.

Shooter V1 → V3
V1 · LM0

Semi-hooded design, two rhino wheels at center, driven by two motors through bevel gears. Great contact with the artifact — but it couldn't shoot far enough.

V2 · LM1

Two flywheels and a straight ramp. Shot much farther and complemented close-shooting alliance partners, but we lost contact time and artifacts didn't launch high enough. Varun helped us brainstorm this direction.

V3 · LM2

Kept the V2 long-range geometry and added a lip as a workaround for the launch-angle problem. Result: a consistent shooter that could still reach the far zone.

Intake 3 revisions
LM0

A row of Gecko wheels driven by a motor and bevel gears. Worked as a ground intake, but couldn't transfer and missed artifacts.

LM1

Mecanum wheels plus Gecko wheels, with a funnel to widen our reach. The mecanums countered artifact rotation and cut transfer inconsistency — but corners were still unreachable and it couldn't hold three at once.

LM2 → Interleague

Switched to a rubber-band intake with a more curved funnel geometry. Held three artifacts and stopped them slipping back out of the ramp.

Transfer V1 → V5
V1

Two custom TPU rollers on a continuous-rotation servo. Delicate, jam-prone, and hard to mount.

V2

A single Gecko wheel on a shaft pushing artifacts into the shooter. Mounted cleanly and transferred well, but often pushed more than one artifact through, with dead spots and compression problems.

V3 · LM1

Back to V1, widened for more contact. More consistent than V1 or V2, but artifacts backflowed into the shooter and the TPU was too malleable.

V4 · LM2

Curved the ends and switched TPU → PLA for stiffness. Better control, no backflow, easy and consistent — but it still occasionally sent two artifacts at once.

V5 · Interleague

A positional servo with a large curved upper that acts as a physical hardstop. While the intake runs, geometry alone prevents an artifact from reaching the shooter — the double-transfer bug is designed out rather than coded around.

Known tradeoffs: the transfer must reverse to reset, which limits cycle time, and it doesn't center the ball entering the shooter, so occasional inconsistency remains.

Method

How we engineer

  1. 1

    Define

    Nail down the intended function and outcome so we can plan against it.

  2. 2

    Brainstorm

    Sketch several ideas and name the early risks — jamming, misalignment — before any prototype exists.

  3. 3

    CAD

    Build it in Onshape and 3D print it. Software ideas get prototyped in code.

  4. 4

    Build & test

    Assemble from the CAD, then record performance and results rather than trusting a feel.

  5. 5

    Iterate

    Analyze, then redesign or keep. This loop is what produced the crescent transfer.

Software

The code that drives it

Java on Android Studio, structured around commands and subsystems. The interesting parts are the control loops.

Architecture

NextFTC CommandBase

An FTC-tailored library where commands, subsystems, and components move every mechanism through simple finite-state-machine logic. Each mechanism is its own subsystem, controlled by commands mapped to gamepad buttons.

Autonomous

Pedro Pathing

A reactive path follower built on Bézier curves. Its built-in PIDF control smooths our motion and corrects for variance in path conditions. We also use it in TeleOp for centripetal force correction and field-centric drive, and because it integrates with NextFTC we can follow paths as commands.

Controls

Custom velocity PIDF + low-pass filter

A consistent shooter needs precise velocity control, so we tuned a PIDF controller using physics, trigonometry, and calculus to respond to the resistance and velocity shifts that happen every time an artifact passes through the flywheels.

On top of the velocity PIDF we run a low-pass filter on measured velocity, averaging past samples with the current one and feeding that as the setpoint. It smooths out the high-frequency oscillation the raw signal caused in the loop.

Vision

Limelight 3A auto-aim

The Limelight detects the AprilTag on the classifier. We compute the heading error between the camera center and the tag center, then run a PIDF controller on the robot's heading to drive it to zero — so drivers stop burning seconds lining up shots.

Localization

Kalman filter sensor fusion

Deadwheels and an IMU on a goBILDA Pinpoint give excellent short-horizon localization, but error accumulates over a 2.5-minute match. We fuse Pinpoint's estimate with pose measurements derived from fixed field AprilTags through a Kalman filter, so we know where we are in TeleOp too — enough to plan a path from any position for an automatic endgame park.

Tooling

Panels dashboard

Live telemetry and graphs. We watch our autonomous path in real time and tune velocities and servo positions without a rebuild cycle.
